1. Strongest supporting evidence

Eivind identifies the ten-seed result in Setting A. Q-learning succeeded in all 2,000 evaluation episodes (100.0 ± 0.0% across ten trained agents); PPO reached 99.4 ± 2.1%, with nine seeds at 100% and one at 93.5%. He considers this the strongest support for a classroom demonstration and evidence that this environment, representation and final budget permit successful learning. It does not separate the algorithms or establish performance in B–D. Trym independently selects the ten perfect Q-learning agents as the strongest finding and regards this as grounds for confidence in a classroom demo in A.

Eivind also notes that Q-learning seeds 0 and 6 reached 0% at some late checkpoints: the final result hides sensitivity to when training stops. The independent training replications are the ten agents, not 2,000 independent training runs; A's 200 evaluation seeds average about 33 distinct trajectories per agent.

2. Claims that went beyond the evidence

Eivind challenges a broad reading of P2 as showing greater PPO seed variation. His short answer accepts A's formal classification, whereas his longer review proposes saying P2 is not supported because A's entire spread comes from one PPO agent and B is inconclusive. The interpretation concern is distinguished from the original classification. The technical check confirms: A is formally supported under the explicit SD threshold (2.1 ≥ 1.5 × 0), while B is inconclusive (17.5/16.7 = 1.05). Greater PPO variability in both settings is not established. No new formal classification is introduced. Trym explicitly retains A's preregistered classification and narrows the conclusion: he would tone down the claim that PPO generally varies more between training runs. This preserves the rule while addressing Eivind's concern about the strength of the interpretation.

Eivind also challenges the classroom explainability argument. Q-values can be inspected, but PPO's action probabilities are visible too. Neither student understanding nor comparative explainability was measured. The brief now states what a student can inspect and treats easier understanding as an untested assumption.

3. A plausible alternative explanation

Eivind proposes that PPO's higher B success reflects the selected Q-learning configuration rather than an intrinsic algorithm advantage. On the matched training seeds 0–4, selected PPO scores 94.3%, selected Q-learning (α 0.3) 67.2%, and Q-learning with only α changed to 0.1 scores 86.4%. The PPO lead shrinks from 27.1 to 7.9 percentage points. All five Q pairs favour α 0.1; α 0.3 seeds 0 and 1 deteriorate in the last 100,000 steps.

He proposes retuning both agents on new tuning seeds, then evaluating new trained agents on fresh evaluation seeds. The current comparison demonstrates configuration sensitivity, not which algorithm is best tuned. Tuning seeds 100–102 favoured α 0.3 (83.7% versus 53.5%, with final ε 0.01 for both). Choosing α 0.1 now using evaluation results would violate comparison rule 7. A future study must keep selection separate from final assessment; crossing the descriptive pooled-SD threshold alone would not establish a causal explanation for a remaining gap. Trym likewise interprets the shrinking gap as sensitivity to the chosen settings. His proposed next experiment gives both methods the same tuning budget, then compares the selected configurations on fresh training seeds. This is future work, not a new experiment claimed in this submission.

4. Comparison with the original predictions

Eivind generally favours retaining the component-wise verdicts and stating what each recommendation depends on. P1 is inconclusive at the specified small budget; P5's ranking/gap predictions and P4a–c are mostly inconclusive. P4d is contradicted: successful episodes shortened by about 2%, not 5%. P3a and the narrower-paddle success drop support the Q-learning predictions, while the corresponding PPO components are inconclusive. P7's predicted between-agent difference is also inconclusive. D had no prior prediction.

The S2 majority-action predictions are contradicted for both agents; PPO's S1 threshold is contradicted, and three of four S3 components are supported. Eivind regards S2's consequences as unresolved. The technical distinction is between preferred action, catch after a forced action and subsequent episode success. Differences below pooled SD do not show practical equivalence; Q-learning seeds 2, 6 and 8 lose 17–40 points after TOWARDS.

P6.2 was triggered: the worst observed PPO B agent scored 39%, versus 46% for Q-learning. Eivind weighs this alongside PPO's higher B mean (87.8% versus 75.9%) and lower failure rate. He supports checking each agent before use when failures matter. These minima are not guaranteed lower bounds, and no acceptance threshold has been established. Trym would try PPO first if the specific trained agent can be tested on fresh episodes before use. Without such testing, he considers the evidence too weak to recommend either method unconditionally when failures matter. This is a conditional preference, not evidence that screening has already been validated.

Eivind's seed-6 GIF observation is retained as supplemental evidence. Existing step logs corroborate a catch followed by stationary FIRE requests in one episode; neither source explains all 122 failures. The original ten episodes are unchanged.
